LECTURE · ACTIVATION FUNCTIONS

From nonlinearity
to learned gating.

Activation functions look like tiny scalar operations. But their continuity, differentiability, smoothness, saturation and gradient behavior shape how an entire neural network learns.

Before memorizing ReLU, GELU, SiLU or SwiGLU, ask a more useful question: what properties do we actually want from an activation function?
01 · THE DESIGN SPACE

What do we want from an activation?

The first requirement is nonlinearity. If every layer were affine, composing many layers would still produce one affine transformation.

\[ f(ax+b)\neq af(x)+b \]

But nonlinearity is only the beginning. A useful activation can also be judged by:

  • Continuity: does the output change without jumps?
  • Differentiability: does a derivative exist where backpropagation needs it?
  • Smoothness: does the function, and perhaps its derivatives, change gently?
  • Gradient behavior: does the derivative vanish or remain useful?
  • Saturation: does the derivative become tiny for large inputs?
  • Output range: bounded or unbounded?
  • Zero-centering: are positive and negative outputs both available?
Key idea. No activation wins every property. Activation design is a sequence of trade-offs.
02 · CONTINUITY

Can an activation jump?

A function is continuous at \(x_0\) when its limiting value agrees with its value:

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0). \]

Continuity is desirable because small changes in the input should not create an arbitrary finite jump in the output. But continuity alone says nothing about whether gradients are usable.

03 · DIFFERENTIABILITY

Can a continuous function still fail to have a derivative?

Yes. The canonical example is:

\[ f(x)=|x|. \]

It is continuous at \(0\), but the slope approaching from the left is \(-1\), while the slope approaching from the right is \(+1\).

This distinction becomes central for ReLU.

04 · SMOOTHNESS

What does “smooth” actually mean?

Informally, smoothness means there are no abrupt changes in the slope. More formally, we can classify functions by differentiability: \(C^0\) means continuous, \(C^1\) means continuously differentiable, and \(C^\infty\) means infinitely differentiable.

Compare ReLU with Softplus:

\[ \operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x), \qquad \operatorname{Softplus}(x)=\log(1+e^x). \]
Important distinction: differentiability, smoothness, and good gradient flow are related but not synonymous.
05 · SATURATION

A function can be perfectly smooth and still be troublesome.

Sigmoid is infinitely differentiable, but its derivative becomes very small in both tails:

\[ \sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}, \qquad \sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x)). \]

Tanh has the same saturation issue, but it is zero-centered:

\[ \tanh(x)\in(-1,1), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh(x)=1-\tanh^2(x). \]
06 · THE RELU FAMILY

Can we avoid saturation on the positive side?

ReLU answers yes:

\[ \operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x). \]

For positive inputs its gradient is \(1\), so it does not saturate there. But the negative side is completely flat.

Leaky ReLU

\[ f(x)=\max(\alpha x,x),\qquad \alpha>0. \]

The negative branch retains a small gradient instead of becoming exactly zero.

PReLU

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} x,&x>0\\ \alpha x,&x\le0 \end{cases} \]

The difference is that \(\alpha\) is learned rather than fixed.

ELU

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} x,&x>0\\ \alpha(e^x-1),&x\le0 \end{cases} \]

ELU keeps a smooth negative branch and approaches \(-\alpha\) for large negative inputs.

SELU

SELU modifies the ELU idea with carefully chosen scaling parameters so that, under specific architectural and initialization assumptions, activations tend toward a self-normalizing regime.

07 · SMOOTH MODERN ACTIVATIONS

If ReLU has a hard corner, can we soften the decision?

Softplus

\[ \operatorname{Softplus}(x)=\log(1+e^x). \]

Softplus is smooth and behaves like a smooth approximation to ReLU.

Swish / SiLU

\[ \operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x\,\sigma(x). \]

The sigmoid factor acts like a soft, input-dependent modulation. Unlike ReLU, the function is smooth and can take slightly negative values.

GELU

\[ \operatorname{GELU}(x)=x\,\Phi(x), \]

where \(\Phi(x)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. GELU smoothly weights an input according to its magnitude rather than applying a hard threshold.

Mish

\[ \operatorname{Mish}(x)=x\tanh(\operatorname{Softplus}(x)). \]

Mish is another smooth, non-monotonic activation. It is useful to know historically and conceptually, even though modern architectures do not uniformly prefer it.

08 · LSTM

Why does an LSTM use sigmoid and tanh?

The answer is architectural role, not generic activation quality.

\[ i_t=\sigma(W_ix_t+U_ih_{t-1}+b_i) \]
\[ f_t=\sigma(W_fx_t+U_fh_{t-1}+b_f) \]
\[ o_t=\sigma(W_ox_t+U_oh_{t-1}+b_o) \]
\[ \tilde c_t=\tanh(W_cx_t+U_ch_{t-1}+b_c) \]

Sigmoid produces values in \((0,1)\), making it naturally useful as a soft gate. Tanh produces a bounded signed candidate state.

\[ c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t, \qquad h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t). \]
Do not memorize: “LSTMs use sigmoid and tanh because they are the best activations.” Instead remember: sigmoid controls; tanh represents a bounded signed state.
09 · GLU FAMILY

What if the activation itself becomes a gate?

A GLU creates two learned paths and multiplies them:

\[ \operatorname{GLU}(x)=A(x)\odot B(x). \]

Modern variants change the activation used on one branch:

VariantFormIdea
GLU\(A\odot\sigma(B)\)sigmoid gating
ReGLU\(A\odot\operatorname{ReLU}(B)\)ReLU gate
GEGLU\(A\odot\operatorname{GELU}(B)\)GELU gate
SwiGLU\(A\odot\operatorname{SiLU}(B)\)smooth learned gate
\[ \operatorname{SwiGLU}(x) = W_3\left(\operatorname{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_2x\right). \]

The evolution in one line:

Nonlinearity → saturation problem → ReLU → dead-negative region → Leaky/PReLU/ELU → smooth nonlinearities → learned multiplicative gating

The practical lesson is not “always use the newest activation.” Choose according to the role the activation must play: generic hidden nonlinearity, bounded output, gate, smooth transformation, or multiplicative interaction.

The useful habit is to look at an activation's graph and immediately ask: Is it continuous? Is it differentiable? Where is its derivative large or small? Does it saturate? Is it zero-centered? What architectural role is it serving?
