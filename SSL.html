SPEECH AI / SELF-SUPERVISED LEARNING

Can speech
teach itself?

We have enormous amounts of speech, but transcripts and speaker labels are expensive. Self-supervised learning asks a different question: what structure can a model discover directly from speech itself?

01 — THE QUESTION

What if the labels
are already in the signal?

A speech recording contains far more information than a transcript tells us.

It contains phonetic structure, lexical information, speaker characteristics, prosody, rhythm, channel characteristics and environmental information.

Yet conventional supervised learning asks us to provide explicit labels for the task we care about.

If the waveform already contains structure, can I construct a learning problem from the waveform itself?

This is the central idea behind speech self-supervised learning.

02 — PREDICTION

The first idea:
predict what comes next.

One way to create a learning signal without labels is to hide the future and ask the model to predict it.

If a representation captures enough information about the past, it should contain useful information about what is likely to happen next.

But what exactly should the model predict? The waveform? Acoustic features? Or a learned representation?

03 — CPC

Contrastive Predictive
Coding.

Contrastive Predictive Coding, or CPC, turns future prediction into a contrastive learning problem.

An encoder transforms the acoustic input into latent representations:

\[ x_t \longrightarrow z_t \]

An autoregressive context network summarizes the past:

\[ z_{1:t} \longrightarrow c_t \]

The model then tries to identify the correct future latent representation among several candidates:

\[ c_t \longrightarrow \hat{z}_{t+k} \]

An InfoNCE-style objective can be written as:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} = -\log \frac{ \exp \left( \operatorname{sim}(c_t,z_{t+k}^{+})/\tau \right) }{ \displaystyle \sum_{z\in\mathcal{Q}_t} \exp \left( \operatorname{sim}(c_t,z)/\tau \right) } \]

The positive example is the actual future representation. The other candidates act as negatives.

Why compare against negatives at all? Why not simply predict the future directly?

04 — APC

Predict the future
without contrastive negatives.

Autoregressive Predictive Coding, or APC, takes a simpler predictive view.

Given acoustic observations up to time \(t\), the model predicts a future acoustic representation.

\[ x_{1:t} \longrightarrow h_t \longrightarrow \hat{x}_{t+k} \]

The prediction can be trained against the actual future acoustic feature using a regression objective.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APC}} = \left\| x_{t+k} - \hat{x}_{t+k} \right\|_2^2 \]

CPC and APC therefore share a basic intuition: future prediction forces the representation to capture useful temporal structure.

But if the target itself is an acoustic feature vector, are we learning the representation we really want?

05 — WAV2VEC

What if we predict
learned speech representations?

The original wav2vec formulation moves closer to the modern speech SSL architecture.

The raw waveform is first processed by a convolutional feature encoder:

\[ x \longrightarrow \mathrm{CNN} \longrightarrow z_{1:T} \]

These latent representations are then processed by a context network:

\[ z_{1:T} \longrightarrow \mathrm{ContextNetwork} \longrightarrow c_{1:T} \]

The model predicts future latent representations using contrastive learning.

But now we have another problem: what should the target representation look like?

06 — QUANTIZATION

What if speech had
discrete latent units?

Quantization maps a continuous latent representation to a discrete entry from a learned codebook.

\[ z_t \longrightarrow \mathrm{Quantizer} \longrightarrow q_t \]

Imagine a codebook containing \(V\) learned vectors:

\[ \mathcal{E} = \left\{ e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_V \right\} \]

The quantizer chooses one or more entries to represent the latent speech representation.

We have therefore introduced a discrete target space instead of asking the model to reconstruct an arbitrary continuous acoustic vector.

But selecting a discrete code is not differentiable. How do we train the codebook end-to-end?

07 — GUMBEL-SOFTMAX

How do we backpropagate
through a discrete choice?

A categorical selection is discrete. A naive argmax would block ordinary gradient propagation.

Gumbel-Softmax provides a differentiable approximation to categorical sampling.

\[ p_v = \frac{ \exp\left((l_v+g_v)/\tau\right) }{ \displaystyle \sum_j \exp\left((l_j+g_j)/\tau\right) } \]

Here \(l_v\) represents the logits, \(g_v\) is Gumbel noise, and \(\tau\) is the temperature.

As the temperature decreases, the distribution becomes increasingly close to a one-hot categorical selection.

A straight-through formulation can use a hard discrete selection during the forward pass while retaining a differentiable approximation for gradient computation.

\[ \text{forward: hard selection} \qquad \text{backward: soft gradient} \]

Now I have a discrete representation. Can I make it useful as a prediction target?

08 — WAV2VEC 2.0

What if we mask
the speech itself?

wav2vec 2.0 makes a major architectural shift: it operates directly on raw waveform input and introduces masking in the latent representation.

\[ x \longrightarrow \mathrm{CNN} \longrightarrow z_{1:T} \]

Some latent time steps are masked before being passed through the Transformer context network.

\[ \tilde{z}_{1:T} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Transformer} \longrightarrow c_{1:T} \]

Meanwhile, the corresponding unmasked latent representation is passed through the quantizer:

\[ z_t \longrightarrow \mathrm{Quantizer} \longrightarrow q_t \]

The Transformer must therefore use surrounding context to identify the correct quantized target at masked positions.

The context network sees the masked sequence, while the target is constructed from the original latent. Can the model recover what was hidden?

09 — PRODUCT QUANTIZATION

One codebook
or several?

wav2vec 2.0 uses product quantization. Instead of choosing one code from one enormous codebook, the representation is divided across multiple codebook groups.

For codebook group \(g\), we have:

\[ \mathcal{E}^{(g)} = \left\{ e_1^{(g)}, e_2^{(g)}, \ldots, e_V^{(g)} \right\} \]

Each group selects a code and the selected entries are combined to construct the quantized representation.

\[ q_t = \operatorname{Concat} \left( e_{v_1}^{(1)}, e_{v_2}^{(2)}, \ldots, e_{v_G}^{(G)} \right) \]

But if the model can keep using only a tiny fraction of the codebook, what stops the representation from collapsing?

10 — THE WAV2VEC 2.0 OBJECTIVE

What exactly
are we optimizing?

At a masked time step, the contextual representation \(c_t\) must identify the correct quantized target \(q_t\) among distractors.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{m} = -\log \frac{ \exp \left( \operatorname{sim}(c_t,q_t)/\kappa \right) }{ \displaystyle \sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_t} \exp \left( \operatorname{sim}(c_t,q)/\kappa \right) } \]

The positive is the correct quantized representation. The remaining candidates act as negatives.

A diversity objective encourages effective use of the available codebook entries.

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{m} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{d} \]

During supervised ASR fine-tuning, the pretrained network can subsequently be optimized using a CTC objective.

The architecture works extremely well. But are randomly sampled negatives always good negatives?

11 — FROM OUR LAB / CCC-WAV2VEC 2.0

Are all negatives
really negative?

CCC-wav2vec 2.0 starts from a very specific observation: speech is quasi-stationary.

Nearby acoustic frames can therefore be highly similar. A randomly selected negative may not be a particularly informative negative.

CCC introduces clustering to identify highly similar representations and reduce the influence of such weak negatives.

\[ z \longrightarrow \mathrm{Clustering} \longrightarrow \text{negative structure} \]

The method also introduces augmentation-based cross-contrastive learning.

The original and augmented versions of the same speech provide two views of the underlying content.

\[ c(x) \leftrightarrow q(\mathcal{A}(x)) \]
\[ c(\mathcal{A}(x)) \leftrightarrow q(x) \]

The idea is therefore to encourage representations to remain consistent across acoustically different views of the same speech.

If augmentation changes the acoustics but should not change the underlying speech representation, shouldn't the SSL objective explicitly enforce that invariance?

CCC-wav2vec 2.0 was introduced by researchers from our lab and accepted at IEEE SLT 2022.

12 — HUBERT

Do we really need
a learned quantizer?

HuBERT takes a strikingly different route.

Instead of learning the prediction targets directly through the quantizer, it creates discrete pseudo-labels using clustering.

\[ \text{acoustic features} \longrightarrow k\text{-means} \longrightarrow y_t \]

The speech representation is then masked and the Transformer is trained to predict the hidden pseudo-labels.

\[ \tilde{x} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Transformer} \longrightarrow p(y_t\mid\tilde{x}) \]

The masked prediction objective can be expressed as:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HuBERT}} = - \sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}} \log p(y_t\mid\tilde{x}) \]

Here \(\mathcal{M}\) is the set of masked positions and \(y_t\) is the cluster-derived pseudo-label.

But where did those pseudo-labels come from? And can better representations produce better labels?

13 — ITERATIVE REFINEMENT

Can the representation
improve its own targets?

This is one of the most interesting ideas in HuBERT.

Initial clustering can produce relatively crude acoustic units. After pretraining, however, the model has learned a richer representation.

Those improved representations can then be clustered again.

\[ \text{features} \rightarrow \text{clusters} \rightarrow \text{pseudo-labels} \rightarrow \text{SSL training} \rightarrow \text{better features} \rightarrow \text{new clusters} \]

What if self-supervised learning is not just learning from labels we already have, but progressively improving the labels themselves?

14 — DATA2VEC

Why make the target
discrete at all?

data2vec takes another conceptual step.

Instead of predicting discrete acoustic units, the student predicts continuous contextual representations produced by a teacher network.

\[ x \longrightarrow \mathrm{Teacher} \longrightarrow y \]
\[ \tilde{x} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Student} \longrightarrow \hat{y} \]

The teacher sees an unmasked view while the student operates on a masked view.

The teacher is updated through an exponential moving average rather than ordinary backpropagation.

\[ \theta_{\mathrm{teacher}} \leftarrow \lambda \theta_{\mathrm{teacher}} + (1-\lambda) \theta_{\mathrm{student}} \]

The objective is therefore representation matching.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data2vec}} = D \left( \hat{y}, y \right) \]

We just spent several models designing better discrete targets. What happens if I remove the discrete bottleneck completely?

15 — FROM OUR LAB / DATA2VEC-AQC

What if discrete and
continuous targets can coexist?

data2vec removes the explicit discrete target. data2vec-AQC asks whether quantization and clustering can still provide useful additional information within the teacher-student framework.

A

Augmentation

Create different acoustic views of the same underlying speech content.

Q

Quantization

Introduce discrete representations as an additional source of structure.

C

Clustering

Discover groups in representation space that can provide additional pseudo-supervision.

OBJECTIVE

Cross-Contrastive Learning

Encourage consistency across the resulting views and representation types.

The interesting part is not simply adding three modules. It is the interaction between them inside the teacher-student framework.

\[ \mathrm{data2vec} + \mathrm{Augmentation} + \mathrm{Quantization} + \mathrm{Clustering} + \mathrm{CrossContrastive} \]

Maybe continuous and discrete representations are not competing descriptions of speech. Maybe they can provide complementary views of it.

data2vec-AQC was introduced by researchers from our lab and accepted at ICASSP 2023.

16 — WAVLM

Should SSL learn
more than phonetics?

A speech representation is not useful only because it knows which phoneme is being spoken.

Downstream tasks may depend on speaker identity, acoustic environment, channel characteristics and other information present in speech.

WavLM extends the masked speech representation framework toward richer speech representations.

A particularly important idea is denoising-oriented training, including mixtures of speech signals and acoustic perturbations.

\[ x_{\mathrm{clean}} + x_{\mathrm{noise}} \longrightarrow \tilde{x} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Encoder} \longrightarrow h \]

Can the representation remain useful when the acoustic conditions change, while still preserving the information required by downstream speech tasks?

17 — WHERE THE IDEA GOES NEXT

Is speech SSL becoming
masked representation learning?

The ideas above connect to a broader family of masked representation learning approaches.

Models such as BEATs and AudioMAE explore related ideas around masked acoustic or audio representation learning.

\[ \text{audio} \longrightarrow \text{mask} \longrightarrow \text{context} \longrightarrow \text{predict representation} \]

The interesting question is no longer simply "what is masked?" It is:

What representation should the model be forced to predict?

19 — WHAT DID WE ACTUALLY LEARN?

A good SSL loss is not
the final objective.

Self-supervised pretraining is useful because the learned representation can later be transferred to downstream tasks.

The representation may encode several factors simultaneously: phonetic information, lexical information, speaker information, prosody and acoustic environment.

Different SSL objectives therefore induce different geometries in representation space.

\[ x \xrightarrow{\text{SSL objective}} h \xrightarrow{\text{downstream task}} \hat{y} \]

If two SSL models achieve similar ASR performance, does that mean they learned the same representation?

Probably not. Downstream performance is only one projection of what the representation has learned.

20 — THE MAP

From predicting the future
to learning speech itself.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Unlabelled speech} &\longrightarrow \text{CPC} \quad \text{future latent prediction} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{APC} \quad \text{future acoustic prediction} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{wav2vec} \quad \text{learned quantized representations} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{wav2vec 2.0} \quad \text{masked quantized prediction} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{CCC-wav2vec 2.0} \quad \text{better negatives + cross-view consistency} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{HuBERT} \quad \text{clustered pseudo-label prediction} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{data2vec} \quad \text{continuous teacher targets} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{data2vec-AQC} \quad \text{continuous + discrete complementary views} \\[12pt] &\longrightarrow \text{WavLM} \quad \text{robust speech representations} \end{aligned} \]

We started with a simple question:

Can speech teach itself?

Perhaps the deeper question is:

What information should a model be forced to preserve when nobody tells it what the answer is?
